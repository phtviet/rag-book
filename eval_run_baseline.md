**Q:** What is quantization?

**A:** Quantization is a technique that reduces a model's numerical precision, which in turn reduces its memory footprint. For example, reducing a model's precision from 32 bits to 16 bits cuts its memory footprint in half. This technique can be applied at different stages:

**Post-training quantization (PTQ)** is performed after a model has been trained in higher precision. It's widely used because it's easy to implement, works well for many models, and is highly effective. Major ML frameworks offer PTQ with just a few lines of code.

**Quantization during training** can serve two purposes:
1. To produce a model that performs well in low precision during inference, addressing potential quality degradation from post-training quantization
2. To reduce training time and cost by lowering the model's memory requirements and speeding up computation

**Quantization-aware training (QAT)** simulates low-precision behavior during training while still performing computations in high precision, allowing the model to learn to produce high-quality outputs in low precision for inference.

Models can also be trained directly in lower precision to achieve both improved inference performance and reduced training costs.

**Q:** What is model distillation?

**A:** Model distillation (also called knowledge distillation) is a method in which a small model (student) is trained to mimic a larger model (teacher). The knowledge of the big model is distilled into the small model, hence the term distillation.

Traditionally, the goal is to produce smaller models for deployment, as deploying a big model can be resource-intensive. Distillation can produce a smaller, faster student model that retains performance comparable to the teacher. For example, DistilBERT reduces the size of a BERT model by 40% while retaining 97% of its language comprehension capabilities and being 60% faster. Similarly, Alpaca, created by finetuning Llama-7B on examples generated by a 175-billion-parameter model, behaves similarly to its teacher while being only 4% the size.

The student model can be trained from scratch or finetuned from a pre-trained model. This technique is particularly useful for creating more efficient models that maintain the capabilities of their larger counterparts while being more practical for deployment.

**Q:** What is RAG?

**A:** RAG stands for retrieval-augmented generation. It is a technique that enhances a model's generation by retrieving relevant information from external memory sources, such as internal databases, a user's previous chat sessions, or the internet.

RAG employs a two-step process: it first retrieves relevant information from external memory and then uses this information to generate more accurate responses. This pattern was originally developed to overcome a model's context limitations, as many tasks require extensive background knowledge that often exceeds a model's context window. For example, code copilots might need access to entire codebases, and research assistants may need to analyze multiple books.

RAG enables more efficient use of information, improving response quality while reducing costs. It allows models to circumvent their context limitations and stay more up-to-date, helping to reduce mistakes and hallucinations that can occur when models are missing necessary context.

**Q:** What are embeddings?

**A:** Embeddings are numerical representations, typically in the form of vectors, that aim to capture the meaning and essence of the original data. They convert complex data into a format that computers can process.

For example, a sentence like "the cat sits on a mat" might be represented as an embedding vector such as [0.11, 0.02, 0.54]. In practice, embedding vectors usually contain between 100 and 10,000 elements.

A key characteristic of good embeddings is that similar data points have closer embeddings when measured by metrics like cosine similarity. For instance, the embedding of "the cat sits on a mat" should be closer to "the dog plays on the grass" than to "AI research is super fun."

While embeddings are commonly used for text, they can represent any type of data, including images, products, graphs, queries, and even users. There are also joint embeddings that can map different types of data (such as text, images, and audio) into a unified embedding space.

Embeddings are produced by specialized models and are used in various applications including classification, topic modeling, recommender systems, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

## Q11 [PASS] (factual)

**Q:** What is backward propagation?

**A:** Backward propagation, also known as backpropagation, is the second phase of a training step in neural networks. It is the process of updating the model's weights using the aggregated signals from the forward pass.

The backward pass works through the following steps:

1. **Computing the loss**: The computed output from the forward pass is compared against the expected output (ground truth). The difference between them is called the loss.

2. **Computing gradients**: The process determines how much each trainable parameter contributes to the mistake by calculating gradients. Mathematically, gradients are computed by taking the derivative of the loss with respect to each trainable parameter. If a parameter has a high gradient, it significantly contributes to the loss and should be adjusted more.

3. **Adjusting parameters**: The trainable parameter values are adjusted using their corresponding gradients. The amount of adjustment for each parameter, given its gradient value, is determined by an optimizer (such as SGD or Adam).

This backward pass is executed during training (along with the forward pass), but not during inference, where only the forward pass is needed.

**A:** Data-centric AI and model-centric AI represent two different approaches to improving AI performance:

**Model-centric AI** focuses on enhancing AI performance by improving the models themselves. This approach involves designing new architectures, increasing model sizes, or developing new training techniques.

**Data-centric AI** focuses on enhancing AI performance by improving the data used to train models. This approach involves developing new data processing techniques and creating high-quality datasets that enable better models to be trained with fewer resources.

In practice, model-centric approaches were more common in the early days of deep learning, where researchers would try to train the best possible model using the same dataset (like ImageNet). More recently, there has been a shift toward data-centric approaches, where researchers work with the same model but try to develop datasets that give the model the best performance through techniques such as fixing incorrect labels, adding edge case examples, and augmenting data.

While this division helps guide research, meaningful technological progress often requires investment in both model and data improvements rather than focusing exclusively on one approach.

**Q:** How does entropy and cross-entropy relate?

**A:** Entropy and cross-entropy are closely related concepts that both measure information content, but they serve different purposes:

**Entropy** measures how much information, on average, a token carries in a language or dataset. It represents the inherent predictability of the data itself - the lower the entropy, the more predictable the language.

**Cross-entropy** measures how difficult it is for a language model to predict what comes next in a dataset. It depends on two factors:
1. The entropy of the training data itself (its inherent predictability)
2. How well the model's learned distribution matches the true distribution of the training data

Mathematically, cross-entropy builds upon entropy through this relationship:

**H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_KL(P || Q)**

Where:
- H(P) is the entropy of the true data distribution
- D_KL(P || Q) is the KL divergence measuring how much the model's distribution diverges from the true distribution
- H(P, Q) is the cross-entropy

This means cross-entropy equals the data's entropy plus an additional penalty for how poorly the model approximates the true distribution. When a model learns perfectly from its training data, the KL divergence becomes zero, and the model's cross-entropy equals exactly the entropy of the training data. In this sense, a model's cross-entropy serves as its approximation of the training data's entropy.

Both metrics use bits as their unit of measurement and are closely interrelated with other metrics like perplexity, bits-per-character, and bits-per-byte.
